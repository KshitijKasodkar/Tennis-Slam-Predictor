00 FAQ — quick answers

Isn't this just the ATP ranking? Ranking is the strongest input, so the top 3 often match. But form, surface record and head-to-head change the order and the odds: in Australia 2023 the model made Djokovic (ranked #5) favourite at 23% when the ranking-only baseline gave him 8% — he won. Across all held-out Slams the model gave champions 28% on average vs 20% for the ranking-only model.

Why is even the favourite only ~28%? A champion must win 7 best-of-five matches. Even at 85% per match that is 0.85⁷ ≈ 32%. Tennis has many upsets, so a calibrated model rarely goes above 50%.

Why train on all ATP events, not just Slams? Slams give ~500 matches a year; all tour-level events give ~2,600. Form and surface records also come from every match a player plays. The best_of_5 feature lets the model treat Slams differently.

Why does the order change between Slams? Surface. Each forecast uses the player's last-24-month win rate on that surface (hard for Melbourne and New York, clay for Paris, grass for London).

Why is the Australian Open forecast the most reliable? It starts ~2 months after our data ends. For Wimbledon and the US Open, months of form, injuries and ranking changes are still to come.

Why log-loss instead of accuracy? We need good probabilities, because title odds multiply seven of them. Log-loss punishes confident wrong predictions; accuracy only checks who was picked.

How do you avoid cheating with future data? Features for a match only use earlier matches; models are tuned on 2010–21, chosen on 2022 and tested once on 2023–24. Past Slam predictions freeze everything before round 1.

What is a "calibrated" probability? When the model says 70%, that player should win about 70% of such matches. The calibration chart in Results checks exactly this.

01 Question

Question. From pre-match information only, how well can we estimate (a) who wins an ATP match and (b) each player's chance of winning a Grand Slam — and does this beat simply trusting the rankings?

  • One row = one completed tour-level match, written Player A vs Player B. Target y=1 if A won.
  • When: just before the match, or before round 1 for title odds (features frozen at the start).
  • Information available then: each player's ATP rank and points as published for that tournament, age, height, and the results of all earlier matches (form, surface record, head-to-head). Nothing from the match itself or later.
  • Useful answer: lower log-loss than the baseline on unseen seasons, calibrated probabilities, more probability on actual champions.
  • Limits: prediction, not causation; ATP men's singles only.
  • Related work: pre-match tennis models range from point-based Markov models [3] to ranking-based regressions and Elo ratings, which a comparison of 11 published models found among the most accurate [4]; machine learning on engineered player features [5]; and surface-specific Elo ratings [6]. We follow the feature-based machine-learning line and add a full tournament simulation on top.

03 Preprocessing & features
  • One chronological pass: read a match's features from the player history, then add its result. No match sees itself or the future, the most common source of leakage in prediction work [17][18].
  • Shrinkage: win rates = (wins + ) / (matches + ) so a 2–0 record isn't “100%”.
  • Coin-flip A/B so the label is balanced and every feature flips sign with it; predictions average both orderings.
  • Imputation/scaling sit inside sklearn pipelines → fitted on training folds only.

04 Baseline & methods

Baseline: rank-only logistic

One feature. What a fan already knows, and in published comparisons ranking-based models are hard to beat [4]. Every model must beat it.

Logistic + Lasso

Additive log-odds, interpretable; L1 drops weak features [7].

SVM (RBF)

Non-linear margin [8]; Platt probabilities [11]; last 12k training rows (O(n²)).

Random forest · XGBoost

Tree ensembles that learn interactions (e.g. form × clay): bagging [9] and gradient boosting [10].

Tuned by 4-fold TimeSeriesSplit on the training years (blocked, past-before-future cross-validation [15]), implemented with scikit-learn [19] and XGBoost [20]:

05 Evaluation design
  • Select on validation log-loss; calibrate (Platt sigmoid [11]) on validation; test once. Sigmoid rather than isotonic calibration, because isotonic overfits when calibration data is small [12].
  • Log-loss is primary: it is a strictly proper scoring rule [14], and title odds multiply ~7 match probabilities, so probability quality matters more than picking winners.
  • Also accuracy, ROC-AUC, Brier score [13], a reliability curve, and a paired bootstrap [16] 95% CI vs the baseline.
  • Tournament level: probability given to the real champion and a bracket log-score, model vs baseline.

What the confidence interval can and cannot tell us

The paired bootstrap resamples the 5,464 test matches, so the interval captures the uncertainty from which matches happened to be in the test seasons. It shows the gain over the baseline is unlikely to be luck of the sample. It does not cover a change of era (the model may work differently in later seasons), and it treats matches as independent even though the same players appear many times, so the true uncertainty is somewhat wider. It says nothing about causes.

08 Limitations
  • Forecast horizon. Form is frozen at . The Australian Open (≈2 months away) is the most trustworthy forecast; Wimbledon and the US Open 2027 are early outlooks.
  • Unknown draws. 2027 forecasts average over random draws with the top 32 on seed lines.
  • Compounding. ~7 match probabilities multiply; matches are treated as independent (no fatigue).
  • Missing context: injuries, withdrawals, motivation, in-tournament form. Example: Jannik Sinner has no matches in the data after Wimbledon 2026, so his ranking in our snapshot is stale (flagged ⚠ on the site). The data has no separate rankings file to refresh it.
  • Small Slam sample ( held-out); one surprise champion moves the scores.
  • No rating system: at Grand Slams, Elo-style ratings (especially surface-specific ones) performed well against the official rankings [6]; adding an Elo feature is the most promising next step.
  • Small tuning grids, fixed before testing and not widened afterwards. Retirements excluded from scoring.
09 Changes from the original plan

The question, the unit of prediction, the features, the models compared, the time split, the metrics and the simulation design are as planned in our project proposal. The main differences, and why:

XGBoost as the main modelRandom forest selected; XGBoost still powers the head-to-head toolWe fixed the rule in advance: lowest validation log-loss wins. Random forest 0.6117 vs XGBoost 0.6120, a statistical tie; overriding the rule would be choosing by preference.
