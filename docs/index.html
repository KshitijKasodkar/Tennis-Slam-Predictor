00 FAQ — quick answers

Isn't this just the ATP ranking? Ranking is the strongest input, so the top 3 often match. But form, surface record and head-to-head change the order and the odds: in Australia 2023 the model made Djokovic (ranked #5) favourite at 23% when the ranking-only baseline gave him 8% — he won. Across all held-out Slams the model gave champions 28% on average vs 20% for the ranking-only model.

Why is even the favourite only ~28%? A champion must win 7 best-of-five matches. Even at 85% per match that is 0.85⁷ ≈ 32%. Tennis has many upsets, so a calibrated model rarely goes above 50%.

Why train on all ATP events, not just Slams? Slams give ~500 matches a year; all tour-level events give ~2,600. Form and surface records also come from every match a player plays. The best_of_5 feature lets the model treat Slams differently.

Why does the order change between Slams? Surface. Each forecast uses the player's last-24-month win rate on that surface (hard for Melbourne and New York, clay for Paris, grass for London).

Why is the Australian Open forecast the most reliable? It starts ~2 months after our data ends. For Wimbledon and the US Open, months of form, injuries and ranking changes are still to come.

Why log-loss instead of accuracy? We need good probabilities, because title odds multiply seven of them. Log-loss punishes confident wrong predictions; accuracy only checks who was picked.

How do you avoid cheating with future data? Features for a match only use earlier matches; models are tuned on 2010–21, chosen on 2022 and tested once on 2023–24. Past Slam predictions freeze everything before round 1.

What is a "calibrated" probability? When the model says 70%, that player should win about 70% of such matches. The calibration chart in Results checks exactly this.

01 Question

Question. From pre-match information only, how well can we estimate (a) who wins an ATP match and (b) each player's chance of winning a Grand Slam — and does this beat simply trusting the rankings?

  • One row = one completed tour-level match, written Player A vs Player B. Target y=1 if A won.
  • When: just before the match, or before round 1 for title odds (features frozen at the start).
  • Useful answer: lower log-loss than the baseline on unseen seasons, calibrated probabilities, more probability on actual champions.
  • Limits: prediction, not causation; ATP men's singles only.

05 Evaluation design
  • Select on validation log-loss; calibrate (Platt sigmoid) on validation; test once.
  • Log-loss is primary: title odds multiply ~7 match probabilities, so probability quality matters more than picking winners.
  • Also accuracy, ROC-AUC, Brier, a reliability curve, and a paired bootstrap 95% CI vs the baseline.
  • Tournament level: probability given to the real champion and a bracket log-score, model vs baseline.

08 Limitations
  • Forecast horizon. Form is frozen at . The Australian Open (≈2 months away) is the most trustworthy forecast; Wimbledon and the US Open 2027 are early outlooks.
  • Unknown draws. 2027 forecasts average over random draws with the top 32 on seed lines.
  • Compounding. ~7 match probabilities multiply; matches are treated as independent (no fatigue).
  • Missing context: injuries, withdrawals, motivation, in-tournament form. Example: Jannik Sinner has no matches in the data after Wimbledon 2026, so his ranking in our snapshot is stale (flagged ⚠ on the site). The data has no separate rankings file to refresh it.
  • Small Slam sample ( held-out); one surprise champion moves the scores.
  • Small tuning grids, fixed before testing and not widened afterwards. Retirements excluded from scoring.
